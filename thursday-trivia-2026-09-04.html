📅 This Day in History — September 4

  • 476 — The Western Roman Empire ends. The Germanic commander Odoacer deposed the teenage emperor Romulus Augustulus at Ravenna. He didn't bother appointing a successor — that's the traditional date historians use for the fall of Rome. 1,550 years ago today.
  • 1781 — Los Angeles is founded. Forty-four settlers ("Los Pobladores") walked over from Mission San Gabriel and founded El Pueblo de Nuestra Señora la Reina de los Ángeles. 245th birthday.
  • 1888 — George Eastman patents the roll-film camera and registers the trademark KODAK — a word he invented because he liked the letter K. It cost $25, came pre-loaded with 100 exposures, and you mailed the whole camera back to Rochester. The slogan: "You press the button, we do the rest."
  • 1957 — Two very different American moments, same day. Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus sent the National Guard to block the Little Rock Nine from entering Central High School. And Ford unveiled the Edsel on "E-Day" after a huge hype campaign; it lasted 26 months and became shorthand for a flop.
  • 2001 — System of a Down releases Toxicity — 25 years ago today. (See the music card.)

📍 Capital Cities

Q: What is the capital of Mongolia?
A: Ulaanbaatar — the coldest national capital on Earth, averaging below freezing year-round. Roughly half of Mongolia's population lives there.

Q: What is the capital of Latvia?
A: Riga — the largest city in the Baltic states, with a UNESCO-listed old town and one of the world's great concentrations of Art Nouveau architecture.

Q: What is the capital of Suriname?
A: Paramaribo — the capital of South America's only Dutch-speaking country; its historic inner city is a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Q: What is the capital of Burkina Faso?
A: Ouagadougou — "Ouaga" for short, and home to FESPACO, the largest film festival in Africa.

🎨 Arts & Literature

Q: Who wrote Crime and Punishment (1866)?
A: Fyodor Dostoevsky — he wrote it under crushing gambling debts, partly to make a deadline that would otherwise have cost him the rights to everything he'd ever written.

Q: Who painted the melting clocks in The Persistence of Memory (1931)?
A: Salvador Dalí — he said the clocks came from watching Camembert melt in the sun.

Q: Which novel opens "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife"?
A: Pride and Prejudice (1813) — Jane Austen

Q: Who composed "Ride of the Valkyries," from the four-opera cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen?
A: Richard Wagner — the full Ring cycle runs about 15 hours across four nights.

Q: Who wrote The Old Man and the Sea (1952)?
A: Ernest Hemingway — Pulitzer in 1953, Nobel in 1954, and it was the last major work published in his lifetime.

Q: Who wrote "Two roads diverged in a yellow wood…" — "The Road Not Taken"?
A: Robert Frost — and it's usually misread; the poem says the two roads were "really about the same."

🏭 Founded & Invented

Q: Who invented the microwave oven, and how?
A: Percy Spencer, Raytheon, 1945 — a candy bar melted in his pocket while he stood near an active magnetron. The first "Radarange" (1947) was nearly six feet tall and weighed about 750 pounds.

Q: What year was Nintendo founded, and what did it originally make?
A: 1889, Kyoto — hanafuda playing cards, by Fusajiro Yamauchi. It also tried taxis, instant rice and love hotels before landing on video games.

Q: Who invented Velcro, and what inspired it?
A: George de Mestral, 1941 — the Swiss engineer pulled burdock burrs off his dog and looked at them under a microscope. The name is velours + crochet.

Q: Who patented riveted denim work pants — i.e., blue jeans?
A: Levi Strauss and tailor Jacob Davis, patent granted May 20, 1873. Davis had the rivet idea; Strauss had the money to file.

Q: Today's date question — who patented the first roll-film camera on Sept. 4, 1888?
A: George Eastman, who also trademarked the invented word "Kodak" the same day.

Q: LocalWho founded Kroger, and where?
A: Barney Kroger, 1883, on Pearl Street in Cincinnati — he sank his entire $372 life savings into it. It's now the largest supermarket chain in the country by revenue.

🧩 General Trivia

  • ScienceHoney never spoils. Archaeologists have found 3,000-year-old honey in Egyptian tombs that was still edible — it's too acidic and too dry for bacteria.
  • SpaceSaturn is less dense than water. Drop it in a big enough bathtub and it would float.
  • GeographyCanada has more lakes than the rest of the world combined — more than two million of them.
  • AnimalsAn octopus has three hearts and blue blood. Two hearts pump to the gills, one to the body — and that one stops when the octopus swims, which is why they prefer to crawl.
  • BodyYou are outnumbered inside your own body: roughly 1.3 bacterial cells for every human cell.
  • FoodPeanuts aren't nuts. They're legumes — closer relatives of peas and beans, and they grow underground.
  • Language"Quarantine" comes from the Italian quaranta giorni — the 40 days ships had to wait offshore during the plague years.
  • HistoryThe Great Pyramid of Giza was the tallest structure humans had ever built for about 3,800 years, until Lincoln Cathedral's spire around 1311.
  • MathShuffle a deck of cards properly and you have almost certainly created an order that has never existed before in history. 52! is about 8 followed by 67 zeros.
  • SportsJames Naismith's original basketball "hoops" were peach baskets — with the bottoms still in. Someone had to climb up and fetch the ball after every made shot.
  • Pop CultureBuzz Lightyear was very nearly named Lunar Larry. Pixar renamed him after astronaut Buzz Aldrin.
  • WeatherFog is mostly nothing. A cubic mile of it contains less than a gallon of actual water.
  • LocalCincinnati's Union Terminal (1933) has the largest half-dome in the Western Hemisphere — and its rotunda was the visual model for the Hall of Justice in Super Friends.
  • Reds bar-betThe first night game in Major League history was played at Cincinnati's Crosley Field on May 24, 1935 — and President Franklin Roosevelt switched the lights on by pressing a button in the White House.
