📅 This Day in History — September 23

  • 1846 — Neptune is discovered, 180 years ago tonight. Urbain Le Verrier worked out where an unseen eighth planet had to be purely from irregularities in Uranus's orbit, mailed the coordinates to Berlin, and Johann Galle found it the same evening within one degree of the prediction. It remains the only planet located by mathematics before anyone looked.
  • 1952 — Richard Nixon's "Checkers" speech. Facing removal from the Republican ticket over a private expense fund, he went on live television, laid out his finances, and admitted to one gift he refused to return: a cocker spaniel his daughters had named Checkers. Roughly 60 million people watched; Eisenhower kept him.
  • 1962 — The Jetsons premieres — ABC's first-ever program broadcast in color. Almost nobody saw it that way; color sets were in well under 5% of American homes.
  • 1845 — The New York Knickerbockers organize and adopt a written rulebook, becoming the first formal baseball club. Foul lines, three outs and the tag rule all trace back to that document.

📍 Capitals Quiz

Capital of Bhutan? Thimphu — one of the only national capitals on Earth with no traffic lights. They installed one once; the public hated it and the white-gloved policeman got his intersection back.

Bolivia has two capitals — name them and the catch. Sucre and La Paz — Sucre is the constitutional capital and home of the Supreme Court; La Paz holds the legislature and the president, which makes it the highest seat of government in the world at roughly 11,900 feet.

Capital of Madagascar? Antananarivo — the name means "city of the thousand," for the thousand soldiers said to have garrisoned it. Locals just call it Tana.

Capital of Croatia? Zagreb — home of the Museum of Broken Relationships, and of a funicular that runs 216 feet, one of the shortest public ones anywhere.

📺 Classic TV — Actors & Roles

Who played Tonto on The Lone Ranger (1949–57)? Jay Silverheels — a Mohawk from Six Nations in Ontario who came to Hollywood as a champion lacrosse player and boxer, and later founded a workshop to train Native American actors.

Who played Eddie Haskell on Leave It to Beaver (1957–63)? Ken Osmond — he left acting to become an LAPD motorcycle officer, and in 1980 was shot three times on duty; his bulletproof vest and belt buckle stopped the rounds.

Who played Admiral Harriman Nelson on Voyage to the Bottom of the Sea (1964–68)? Richard Basehart — he later narrated Knight Rider's opening titles, uncredited.

Who played Huggy Bear on Starsky & Hutch (1975–79)? Antonio Fargas — the character was popular enough that ABC shot a spin-off pilot for him that never got picked up.

Who played Balki Bartokomous on Perfect Strangers (1986–93)? Bronson Pinchot — he invented Balki's accent and his home country of Mypos out of nothing; he'd been Serge in Beverly Hills Cop two years earlier.

Who played Amanda Woodward on Melrose Place (1992–99)? Heather Locklear — brought in as a four-episode guest to rescue the ratings, she stayed seven years and got the "special guest star" billing the whole time.

🎨 Arts & Literature

Who painted American Gothic (1930)? Grant Wood — the models were his sister Nan and his dentist, Dr. Byron McKeeby, and Wood always insisted they were a farmer and his daughter, not his wife.

Who wrote Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937)? Zora Neale Hurston — written in about seven weeks while she was doing fieldwork in Haiti. She died in obscurity in 1960; Alice Walker tracked down her unmarked grave in 1973 and paid for a headstone.

"Because I could not stop for Death — / He kindly stopped for me" — which poet? Emily Dickinson — fewer than a dozen of her roughly 1,800 poems were published while she was alive, and those were edited without her consent.

Who composed Boléro (1928)? Maurice Ravel — one crescendo, the same 18-bar theme repeated over and over. He described it as "a piece for orchestra without music" and was baffled that audiences loved it.

Who wrote Death of a Salesman (1949)? Arthur Miller — he built a small studio by hand in Connecticut, then wrote the first act in a single day. It won the Pulitzer that year.

Who sculpted The Thinker? Auguste Rodin — originally titled The Poet and meant to be Dante, seated above The Gates of Hell contemplating the whole thing.

🏭 Founded & Invented

What was bubble wrap originally invented to be? Wallpaper — Alfred Fielding and Marc Chavannes sealed two shower curtains together in 1957 to make textured wall covering. Nobody wanted it. They tried greenhouse insulation next. IBM finally used it to ship the 1401 computer in 1960.

Who invented the Post-it Note? Spencer Silver and Art Fry, 3M — Silver made a famously weak adhesive in 1968 and spent years looking for a use; Fry wanted a bookmark that would stay in his church hymnal. National launch came in 1980.

When was LEGO founded, and where does the name come from? 1932, Ole Kirk Christiansen, Billund, Denmark — from the Danish leg godt, "play well." The interlocking plastic brick as we know it wasn't patented until 1958.

Who invented the Slinky? Richard James, 1943 — a naval engineer testing springs to stabilize shipboard instruments knocked one off a shelf and watched it walk. His wife Betty found the name in a dictionary; 400 sold in 90 minutes at Gimbels in Philadelphia.

When was the barcode first scanned in a store? June 26, 1974 — a 10-pack of Wrigley's gum in Troy, Ohio — the patent went to Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver back in 1952; Woodland's idea came from dragging his fingers through sand and stretching Morse code into lines.

LocalPlay-Doh started life as a Cincinnati product — doing what? Cleaning wallpaper — Kutol Products of Cincinnati made a pliable putty for lifting coal soot off wallpaper. When homes switched to gas heat the business collapsed, so the McVicker family rebranded it as a modeling toy in the mid-1950s.

🧩 General Trivia

  • SpaceThe footprints on the Moon will outlast everyone reading this. No wind, no water, no weather — only micrometeorite dust slowly sanding them down. Estimates run into the millions of years.
  • BodyFingernails grow about four times faster than toenails — and faster on your dominant hand, because use drives blood flow to the nail bed.
  • GeographyAfrica is the only continent in all four hemispheres — north and south of the equator, east and west of the prime meridian.
  • FoodKetchup was sold as medicine. In the 1830s an Ohio physician marketed "tomato pills" as a cure for indigestion, diarrhea and jaundice. The craze collapsed when competitors started selling laxatives under the same label.
  • Language"Set" carries more distinct definitions than any other English word — the Oxford English Dictionary gives it well over 400 senses, and the entry took one editor months.
  • AnimalsSea otters hold hands while they sleep so the current doesn't separate them. Mothers will also wrap pups in kelp to anchor them while they dive.
  • ScienceWater is one of the very few substances that expands when it freezes — about 9%. That's why ice floats, why lakes freeze from the top down, and why anything living under them survives the winter.
  • HistoryAnne Frank and Martin Luther King Jr. were born in the same year — 1929. So were Audrey Hepburn and Grace Kelly.
  • SportsAn Olympic gold medal is mostly silver. The rules require at least 92.5% silver with about six grams of gold plating. The last solid gold medals were handed out in 1912.
  • Pop CultureMonopoly's "Marvin Gardens" is a typo. The real place outside Atlantic City is Marven Gardens — a portmanteau of Margate and Ventnor. Parker Brothers formally apologized in 1995, then left the board alone.
  • NatureRaindrops are not teardrop-shaped. Small ones are spheres; bigger ones flatten into something closer to a hamburger bun as air pushes up from below, and past about 4mm they break apart.
  • LocalCincinnati has an abandoned subway beneath Central Parkway — roughly two miles of finished tunnel and a handful of stations, built between 1920 and 1925, funding gone by 1927, and not one passenger ever carried. It is the largest abandoned subway system in the United States.
  • Reds bar-betThe first night game in major league history was played in Cincinnati — May 24, 1935, at Crosley Field. Franklin Roosevelt threw the switch by telegraph key from the White House, and the Reds beat the Phillies 2–1.
