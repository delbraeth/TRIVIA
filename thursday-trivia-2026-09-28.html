📅 This Day in History — September 28

  • 1928 — Alexander Fleming finds penicillin in a dirty dish. Back at St Mary's in London after a holiday in Scotland, Fleming noticed a mould had contaminated one of his Staphylococcus plates and killed the bacteria in a ring around itself. He wrote it up and largely moved on; it took another decade and a different team at Oxford to turn it into a drug. 98 years ago today.
  • 1941 — Ted Williams refuses to sit on .400. Going into the last day of the season Williams was batting .3995, which the record books would have rounded up to .400. He played both games of a doubleheader in Philadelphia anyway, went 6-for-8, and finished at .406. Nobody has hit .400 since. 85 years ago today.
  • 1972 — Paul Henderson scores with 34 seconds left. Game 8 of the Summit Series in Moscow, Canada down in the series, Henderson stuffs in his own rebound to win it 6–5. Canadian schools and offices stopped to watch; it is still the most replayed goal in the country's history.

Also today: American and French forces began the siege of Yorktown in 1781 — 245 years ago; three U.S. Army planes landed in Seattle in 1924 to complete the first round-the-world flight, 175 days out; the ferry MS Estonia sank in the Baltic in 1994, killing 852; Rabin and Arafat signed the Oslo II accord at the White House in 1995; and in 2008 SpaceX's Falcon 1 became the first privately developed liquid-fueled rocket to reach orbit, on the company's fourth and nearly final attempt.

🌍 Capitals Quiz

What is the capital of Liberia? — Monrovia (named for U.S. President James Monroe — the only foreign capital named after an American president. The settlement was founded in 1822 by freed American slaves.)

What is the capital of Kosovo? — Pristina (Europe's newest capital: Kosovo declared independence in 2008 and is still not universally recognized. It also has the youngest population in Europe by median age.)

What is the capital of Tuvalu? — Funafuti (one of the smallest and lowest-lying countries on Earth. A meaningful slice of its national income comes from licensing its internet domain — .tv — to broadcasters and streamers.)

What is the capital of Mauritius? — Port Louis (Mauritius is the island where the dodo lived — and where it was wiped out within roughly a century of the first Dutch landing. Mark Twain reported being told that heaven was copied after Mauritius.)

📺 Classic TV — Actors & Roles

Who played veterinarian Dr. Marsh Tracy on Daktari (1966–69)? — Marshall Thompson (the Kenya-set series was shot entirely at Africa USA, an animal ranch north of Los Angeles; the real stars were Clarence the Cross-Eyed Lion and Judy the chimp)

Who played Capt. Steve Burton on Irwin Allen's Land of the Giants (1968–70)? — Gary Conway (at roughly $250,000 an episode it was the most expensive series on television at the time — all those oversized props)

Who played undercover detective Tony Baretta on Baretta (1975–78)? — Robert Blake ("Don't do the crime if you can't do the time." His partner was a sulphur-crested cockatoo named Fred.)

Who played housekeeper Nell Harper on Gimme a Break! (1981–87)? — Nell Carter (she had already won a Tony for Ain't Misbehavin'; the show was also Joey Lawrence's first regular role)

Who played the title character on MacGyver (1985–92)? — Richard Dean Anderson (his first name — Angus — was deliberately withheld and not revealed until the seventh season)

Who played Dick Solomon on 3rd Rock from the Sun (1996–2001)? — John Lithgow (three Emmys for the role; he was already a two-time Oscar nominee when he took a sitcom about aliens)

🎨 Arts & Literature

Which American painter caused a Paris scandal with Portrait of Madame X (1884)? — John Singer Sargent (one fallen shoulder strap was enough; he repainted it upright, and left Paris for London anyway. He later called it the best thing he ever did.)

Which Finnish composer wrote Finlandia (1899)? — Jean Sibelius (written as covert protest against Russian censorship; to get it past the censors it was performed under bland disguises like "Happy Feelings at the Awakening of Finnish Spring")

Which Nobel laureate wrote The Remains of the Day and Never Let Me Go? — Kazuo Ishiguro (born in Nagasaki, moved to England at five, Nobel Prize in Literature 2017)

Which playwright wrote the ten-play "Pittsburgh Cycle," one play for each decade of the 20th century? — August Wilson (Pulitzers for Fences and The Piano Lesson; nine of the ten are set in the Hill District where he grew up)

Which Chilean poet won the 1971 Nobel Prize in Literature? — Pablo Neruda (a pen name — he was born Ricardo Eliécer Neftalí Reyes Basoalto, and most likely borrowed the surname from Czech writer Jan Neruda)

Which sculptor invented the mobile? — Alexander Calder (a trained mechanical engineer. Marcel Duchamp is the one who gave the hanging pieces the name "mobiles" in 1931.)

🏭 Founded & Invented

Who invented photocopying? — Chester Carlson, 1938 (a patent clerk tired of copying documents by hand. He made the first xerographic image — "10-22-38 ASTORIA" — in a rented room in Queens, then spent six years being turned down by more than twenty companies, including IBM and GE. Little Haloid Company licensed it in 1947 and eventually renamed itself Xerox.)

What prompted the instant camera? — A three-year-old's question, 1943 (Edwin Land's daughter asked on holiday why she couldn't see the photo he had just taken. He worked out the chemistry on a walk that afternoon, demonstrated it in 1947, and Polaroid sold the first camera in 1948.)

How long did it take to invent Trivial Pursuit? — About 45 minutes. (On December 15, 1979, two Canadian newspapermen — photo editor Chris Haney and sportswriter Scott Abbott — sat down to play Scrabble, found pieces missing, and sketched a trivia game instead.)

How old was Colonel Sanders when he started franchising KFC? — 62 (a new interstate had routed traffic away from his Corbin, Kentucky restaurant. And the "Colonel" is not military — it is an honorary Kentucky Colonel commission handed out by the governor.)

How did L.L. Bean's famous guarantee start? — A disaster, 1912 (Leon Leonwood Bean's first Maine Hunting Shoe had a leather top stitched to a rubber bottom. The stitching failed: 90 of the first 100 pairs came back. He refunded every one, nearly went under, and turned the refund into company policy.)

Local: Why does Kings Island exist? — The Ohio River kept flooding Coney Island. (Taft Broadcasting moved the whole operation 24 miles northeast and opened April 29, 1972. The Racer is widely credited with restarting the American roller coaster industry, and The Beast — 1979 — is still the longest wooden coaster in the world.)

🧩 General Trivia

  • SpaceAstronauts on the International Space Station circle the Earth about every 90 minutes at roughly 17,500 mph — which means they watch 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day.
  • ScienceTungsten has the highest melting point of any metal: 6,192°F. That is the entire reason it is the wire inside an old-fashioned light bulb.
  • GeographyFour Corners is the only place in the United States where four states meet — and the 1875 survey put the marker a few hundred feet off from where it was supposed to go. It doesn't matter: the legal rule is that wherever the surveyors set the monument is the boundary. The mistake is the border.
  • AnimalsA giraffe has exactly seven vertebrae in its neck — the same number you have. Each one is just about ten inches long.
  • BodyHiccups are probably an evolutionary leftover from gill-breathing ancestors. Charles Osborne of Iowa hiccupped continuously from 1922 to 1990 — 68 years — and they stopped on their own a year before he died.
  • FoodDurian is banned on Singapore's subway. The fruit gets its own prohibition sign — and it is the only sign on the network with no fine printed underneath it, apparently because nobody thought enforcement would ever be necessary.
  • Language"Boycott" is a man's name. Captain Charles Boycott was a land agent in County Mayo whose tenants, shopkeepers and even his postman refused to deal with him in 1880. Within months newspapers were using his surname as a verb.
  • HistoryThe Pony Express lasted 18 months. It started in April 1860 and shut down in October 1861 — two days after the transcontinental telegraph was completed. It is remembered far better than it ever performed, and it lost money the entire time.
  • MathEurope did not really use zero until the 1200s. Fibonacci introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals in Liber Abaci in 1202, and Florence banned them in 1299 — partly because a 0 was too easy to doctor into a 6 or a 9.
  • SportsIn tennis, "love" means zero, most likely from the French l'œuf — "the egg" — for the shape of a nought. The Oxford English Dictionary prefers the other theory: playing "for love," meaning for nothing.
  • Pop CultureThe cat Marlon Brando strokes in the opening scene of The Godfather was a stray Coppola found wandering the Paramount lot. It wasn't in the script, and it purred so loudly the sound crew worried they'd have to redo Brando's dialogue.
  • WeatherThe hottest air temperature ever officially recorded is 134°F, at Furnace Creek in Death Valley on July 10, 1913. Meteorologists have been arguing about whether that reading is real for more than a century.
  • LocalCincinnati's American Sign Museum in Camp Washington is the only public museum of its kind in the country — an indoor "Main Street" of restored neon, porcelain and painted signs, plus a working neon shop on site.
  • Reds bar-betEric Davis homered in his first World Series at-bat — a two-run shot off Dave Stewart in the first inning of Game 1, 1990, which set the tone for the Reds' sweep of a 103-win Oakland team. In Game 4 he lacerated a kidney diving for a ball, the club left him in an Oakland hospital, and he paid his own way home — which is how his famous feud with owner Marge Schott started.
