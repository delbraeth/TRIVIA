📅 This Day in History — September 7

  • 1936 — The last known thylacine dies. "Benjamin," the last Tasmanian tiger in captivity, died at Hobart's Beaumaris Zoo after being locked out of its shelter overnight. 90 years ago today — and the reason Australia now marks September 7 as National Threatened Species Day. The species was granted legal protection 59 days before the last one died.
  • 1979 — ESPN launches. The Entertainment and Sports Programming Network went on the air from Bristol, Connecticut, founded by fired Hartford Whalers employee Bill Rasmussen. The first-ever broadcast was SportsCenter; early programming leaned on slow-pitch softball and Australian rules football because nobody would sell them real rights.
  • 1813 — "Uncle Sam" enters the language. The nickname for the United States appeared in print in the Troy Post, traditionally traced to Samuel Wilson, a Troy, New York meatpacker who stamped Army barrels "U.S."
  • 1533 — Elizabeth I is born at Greenwich Palace — a daughter, to Henry VIII's considerable disappointment. She ruled for 44 years.

📍 Capital Cities

Q: What is the capital of Cuba?
A: Havana — founded in 1519, the largest city in the Caribbean, and its old town has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1982.

Q: What is the capital of Albania?
A: Tirana — a former mayor turned prime minister, Edi Rama, had the grey communist-era apartment blocks painted in wild colors as a civic experiment.

Q: What is the capital of Malta?
A: Valletta — built by the Knights of St. John after the 1565 siege; the entire city, all 0.3 square miles of it, is a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Q: What is the capital of Mozambique?
A: Maputo — known as Lourenço Marques until independence in 1975; its central railway station was designed in the era of Gustave Eiffel's firm.

🎨 Arts & Literature

Q: Who painted The Kiss (1907–08), the gold-leaf embrace that defines Vienna's "Golden Phase"?
A: Gustav Klimt — he used actual gold leaf, a technique he learned from his father, a gold engraver.

Q: Who wrote Lord of the Flies (1954)?
A: William Golding — a schoolteacher at the time; the manuscript was rejected by roughly 20 publishers. Nobel Prize, 1983.

Q: Who composed the Messiah, including the "Hallelujah" chorus?
A: George Frideric Handel — he wrote the whole thing in about 24 days in 1741, and it premiered in Dublin, not London.

Q: Which 1922 novel follows Leopold Bloom through a single day in Dublin — June 16, now celebrated as Bloomsday?
A: Ulysses — James Joyce

Q: Who wrote the 1969 memoir I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, and read a poem at Bill Clinton's 1993 inauguration?
A: Maya Angelou — the poem was "On the Pulse of Morning."

Q: Who painted the three-panel triptych The Garden of Earthly Delights?
A: Hieronymus Bosch — around 1500, and nobody has ever fully agreed on what it means.

🏭 Founded & Invented

Q: Who invented the shopping cart, and why?
A: Sylvan Goldman, 1937, Oklahoma City — a grocer who noticed customers stopped shopping once their basket got heavy. Shoppers refused to use it at first (men thought it looked weak, women thought it looked like a baby carriage), so he hired actors to push carts around his stores.

Q: Who invented the Wiffle Ball?
A: David Mullany, 1953, Fairfield, Connecticut — an out-of-work semi-pro pitcher who cut oblong holes in a plastic packaging sphere so his son could throw a curveball. The name came from "whiff."

Q: Who invented Tupperware, and who actually made it a phenomenon?
A: Earl Tupper, 1946 — but Brownie Wise invented the home party. It sold terribly in stores; she became the first woman on the cover of Business Week, then Tupper fired her.

Q: Who invented the automated teller machine?
A: John Shepherd-Barron — first installed at a Barclays in Enfield, London, in 1967. He wanted a six-digit PIN; his wife said she could only remember four.

Q: The Rubik's Cube was invented in 1974 by a Hungarian professor — of what?
A: Architecture — Ernő Rubik built it as a teaching model for three-dimensional geometry. It took him a month to solve his own puzzle.

Q: LocalWhat legendary record label did Syd Nathan found in Cincinnati in 1943?
A: King Records — Evanston, Cincinnati. It recorded James Brown's Live at the Apollo, ran a racially integrated staff and pressing plant in the 1940s, and put out country and R&B under the same roof.

🧩 General Trivia

  • ScienceGallium is a metal that melts at 85°F — hold a chunk in your palm and it turns into a silver puddle. It's the basis of the classic "disappearing spoon" prank.
  • SpaceThe Sun accounts for 99.86% of all the mass in the solar system. Everything else — all eight planets, every moon, asteroid and comet — is the rounding error.
  • GeographyThe shore of the Dead Sea is the lowest dry land on Earth, about 1,410 feet below sea level — and it's dropping roughly three feet every year as the sea shrinks.
  • BodyPound for pound, the strongest muscle in the human body is the masseter — your jaw muscle.
  • Language"Nice" originally meant ignorant, from the Latin nescius. It drifted through "foolish," "fussy," and "precise" before landing on "pleasant."
  • HistoryThe Statue of Liberty was a shiny copper-penny brown when it opened in 1886. It took about 30 years of oxidation to turn green, and when the Park Service considered polishing it, the public objected.
  • MathThe word "googol" — 1 followed by 100 zeros — was coined by a nine-year-old. Mathematician Edward Kasner asked his nephew Milton Sirotta to name a very big number.
  • SportsGolf was banned in Scotland in 1457 by King James II, because men were playing it instead of practicing archery. The ban was repeated in 1471 and 1491 — which tells you how well it worked.
  • Pop CultureBob Ross painted every Joy of Painting piece three times: once beforehand as a reference, once on camera, and once afterward in detail for the companion books.
  • WeatherThe largest hailstone ever recorded in the U.S. fell on Vivian, South Dakota, in July 2010 — eight inches across, nearly two pounds, and roughly the size of a volleyball.
  • FoodThe reason your tongue tingles after too much fresh pineapple: bromelain, an enzyme that digests protein. Briefly, the pineapple is eating you back.
  • AnimalsA group of jellyfish is a "smack." A group of porcupines is a "prickle." A group of ferrets is a "business."
  • LocalCincinnati founded the first fully paid, full-time professional fire department in the United States in 1853 — driven partly by the fact that the volunteer companies kept brawling with each other at fire scenes.
  • Reds bar-betJohnny Bench could hold seven baseballs in one hand — a party trick he did on camera, and a fair explanation for how he handled a catcher's mitt.
